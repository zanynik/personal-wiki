AI and Human Flourishing

The most compelling question about artificial intelligence today isn't whether the models are impressive—it's whether they actually expand our capacity to think, act, and cooperate. In my notes from recent months, I keep returning to a single fork in the road: AI can become a prosthetic for human agency, or it can settle in as another layer of dependence that quietly narrows the space of our lives.

IntelligenceShould Lower Friction for Thought

A durable claim emerging from recent conversations is that AI can turn large swaths of deterministic code into reusable mental scaffolds. When a programmer asks an LLM to stitch together well‑defined functions, the model does the low‑level plumbing, leaving the human free to focus on higher‑order design decisions. This reduces friction not by eliminating thought but by shifting the effort from routine syntax to strategic reasoning.

The tension, however, is between efficiency and cognitive depth. If every routine step is automated, the practitioner may lose the “muscle memory” that grounds intuition. Conversely, keeping too much manual work re‑introduces the very friction AI could alleviate. The question becomes: how much deterministic scaffolding should be handed over before the tool starts to dull the thinker’s edge?

Examples illustrate the spectrum.

  • Adaptive code assistants that generate boilerplate instantly lower entry barriers for newcomers, democratizing access to complex systems.
  • Deterministic pipelines that auto‑compose data‑flow graphs can obscure the underlying logic, making it harder for users to audit or modify the process later.
  • Personal workflow challenges: A user’s experience with the Codex app highlights friction in AI reliability—sync issues between iCloud and Google Drive delay essay updates, forcing manual intervention. This raises questions about whether AI tools can consistently lower friction or if their practical limitations reintroduce cognitive load.

These cases raise a second tension: curation versus accessibility. An AI that simply serves any request maximizes accessibility but may flood the user with unchecked suggestions. A more curated approach—where the system surfaces only a few, deliberately diverse alternatives—preserves space for critical reflection.

Open question: Should AI‑driven environments prioritize seamless efficiency, or should they purposefully introduce “friction points” (e.g., prompting users to explain why a generated snippet fits their intent) to sustain human agency and critical thinking? The author’s struggle with the Codex app—where automation falters due to sync delays and the need for manual cleanup—suggests that AI’s value may depend on its ability to adapt to human workflows rather than impose rigid efficiency. Could systems that ask for human input during contradictions or outliers (as the author proposes) better balance automation with the preservation of thoughtful engagement?

A More Capable Interface To Knowledge

Recent prototyping of the “slocal” slow‑AI journaling app shows that a locally‑run Gemma model can reliably turn text or audio notes into structured blog posts and keep a personal website up‑to‑date, but its current workflows surface several tensions. Summarizing journal nodes and extracting actionable tasks already work, yet the app’s reliance on full photo‑library access raises privacy concerns and its limited video‑processing means users must prioritize text and audio inputs for now. For example, a user can record a day’s events via voice, have the AI generate a concise narrative, and automatically scan a photo of a calendar to create scheduled tasks. However, ongoing bugs—especially around authentication and local model deployment—highlight the need for a more robust login system and clearer model‑update mechanisms before the interface can scale beyond these core use cases.

The Economic Question Cannot Be Avoided

The rise of AI is reshaping the very fabric of economic life, and it cannot be discussed in isolation from questions of income distribution, labor markets, and social welfare. The 2026 audio journal recorded at 07:50 h on February 26th captures a moment of reckoning: “something really big is happening… industrial and the computer revolution.” The speaker notes that the computer revolution of the 1970s already produced a sharp increase in income inequality, as wages for routine workers fell while capital‑intensive firms grew. That historical precedent suggests that AI, as the next wave of automation, will follow a similar trajectory unless deliberate policy measures intervene.

Durable claims that emerge from this observation are:

1. AI will accelerate the displacement of routine, middle‑skill jobs in ways that mirror the 1970s computer revolution, pushing wages lower for those whose tasks can be algorithmically replicated.

2. Capital concentration will deepen because firms that own and deploy AI systems can scale production without commensurate increases in labor input, thereby widening the gap between high‑income owners and low‑income workers.

3. Policy responses must be proactive—taxes on automated labor, universal basic income pilots, and retraining programs—if society is to avoid a repeat of the stark inequality that followed the industrial and computer revolutions.

These points frame the economic question as a central, unavoidable component of the broader debate about AI and human flourishing.

Tools Should Increase Agency, Not Replace It

The current landscape shows a shift toward tools that empower personal agency rather than automate it away. The real test lies in how these innovations support human decision-making and self-expression. I've noticed this tension in my own work building systems at the intersection of AI, philosophical economics, and long-term projects: the impulse to use technology as a lever for transforming structures, not as a substitute for the thinking that gives those structures meaning. When I consider writing a book, for instance, the question isn't whether AI can make it easier—it's whether ease comes at the cost of not knowing how every line is structured, what it means, and how it forms. If something is worth producing, the work of shaping it line by line isn't friction to be removed; it's where the agency lives. The same principle applies to any tool that promises to "help" by taking over the generative act: does it leave me more capable of directing the outcome, or does it quietly assume the role of author?
