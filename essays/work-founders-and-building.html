Work, Founders, and Building

Most people know what they want to build. The harder problem is keeping it alive long enough to finish. That gap—between intention and momentum—is where I have been living lately. The answer is not more motivation. It is structure, the kind that corporate work provides almost by accident: documents, tickets, boards, visible next steps, and a rhythm that carries you forward even on days when inspiration is absent.

Work Gives Shape Even When It Is Not the Goal

A company rarely depends on inspiration; it depends on rhythm. The cadence of tickets, Kanban boards, and deadlines keeps projects moving whether we feel like it or not. Ordinary work can feel easier to sustain than self‑directed work, even when the latter is far more meaningful, because the former is scaffolded by visible structure.

I saw this firsthand when I fixed the feed algorithm in Opus 4.6. The problem was identified automatically by the system’s log, and the fix emerged from a single terminal command. The work was not about passion; it was about process: logging the issue, reproducing it, and applying a mathematical speed formula that the algorithm had been missing. The rhythm of logging and iteration made the solution feel inevitable.

That rhythm does not make bureaucracy beautiful; it makes scaffolding matter. The challenge is to extract the minimum viable structure from professional life without importing its dead weight. I tried to apply that lesson to my own life by treating myself as both project manager and executor: I documented everything, let to‑dos surface from notes, and used Kanban to keep focus. The result was a prototype that could clean and organize my documentation, approve or reject snippets, and push relevant pieces to social platforms. The project stalled because it had to run on a phone, where I could capture thoughts in real time. The tension remains: how much structure serves the individual without becoming its own form of busywork?

The same tension shows up in the feed I am building. After the algorithm was fixed, the next step was to think about UI and packaging. I want a feed that behaves like a familiar social‑media experience—blue‑sky‑style with clear categories for tweets, videos, articles, and news. The feed should render a preview of news articles and open them in a browser, or even better, in an in‑app viewer that preserves the user’s context. At the same time, I need to package the app so that it works even without a ChatGPT subscription. One approach is to let users set a limit on AI usage per day, similar to how some sites throttle API calls. Users could journal their preferences—“cat videos,” “philosophy,” “news about India”—and the AI would tailor the feed accordingly. The question is whether to build on Nostra, with its custom algorithms, or on Blue Sky, with its open‑source protocol. Each platform offers a different balance of flexibility and community support.

In short, work gives shape because it forces us to make choices about structure, even when the goal is not to build a product. The same structure that keeps a company running can also keep a personal project alive, but only if we are careful not to let it become its own burden.

Build First for the Real User in Front of You

The entrepreneurial instinct in all of this is strongest when it stops pretending to design for everyone. The best product ideas begin as honest self-use cases: a tool for managing notes, a system for turning raw thought into action, a more humane way to organize side projects, or a repository that AI can actually work with. That is a much healthier starting point than abstract startup ambition.

What emerges is a founder ethic grounded in lived friction. Build the thing that removes a real bottleneck you encounter daily. Keep it small enough to stay honest. Let the system earn its complexity only after it proves useful. I do not need to scale the idea yet. I need to finish the thing I am already reaching for.

For instance, after wrestling with a broken feed algorithm for weeks, the solution came not from abstract optimization but from deploying Opus 4.6 to log and diagnose the issue directly in my terminal. Now that the core functionality works, the next friction point is clear: the UI needs to feel familiar, like Blue Sky's social interface, with distinct spaces for quick posts, videos, and articles. But how do I surface the right content without requiring users to have ChatGPT subscriptions? The answer might lie in letting people journal their preferences—"I want cat videos and philosophy news"—and using that as the initial feed algorithm.

This raises a tension: Nostra offers custom algorithms, but Blue Sky has a mature social framework. Should I build on existing infrastructure or carve out my own path? The question isn't scalability yet—it's whether the app works for someone who just wants to see their interests reflected, not a startup pitch.

In my own journey, I've discovered that the most practical path forward is building an iPhone app to distribute my creation among friends. For me to use it regularly, I need it on my device—currently I'm using voice memos for recordings. The core functionalities need hardening: the whisper translation that uses a local model on my desktop, and ensuring it works effectively on mobile. Most importantly, the feed experience must be designed as a social media interface from the start, allowing users to simply record what they want to see. This creates a natural journaling process where users' thoughts and preferences directly shape their content experience without requiring complex engineering. By recording all user data—audio journals, text notes, video reviews—we can create a truly personalized system that reflects what matters most to each individual user.

Yet the path forward is not without its own friction. My current workflow—daily voice recordings synced through a fragile chain of iCloud to Google Drive—breaks down under the weight of its own inefficiency. This unreliability saps motivation, turning what should be a seamless creative loop into a monthly chore. The AI agent I once imagined, always-on and capable of surfacing contradictions in my thoughts, now feels both necessary and elusive. If the system could quietly generate content until it detects an outlier—a philosophical tension, an unmet need—then alert me only when my attention is truly required, perhaps the journaling process would feel less like a burden and more like a dialogue. But how do I build that without losing the intimacy of the practice itself? The answer may lie in accepting that the tool must first work flawlessly for me, before it can work for anyone else.

PersonalSystems Should Be Built With The Same Care As Company Systems

Building personal systems with rigor and intention mirrors the discipline required to scale a company. Just as founders must design processes that endure growth, individuals must craft routines that withstand the friction of daily life.

Consider meditation: the practice isn’t about emptying the mind but mastering it. When thoughts arise, the skill lies in observing them without engagement—training focus to activate only when deliberately directed. This same precision applies to personal systems. A task manager isn’t just a tool; it’s a framework for channeling attention. A content workflow isn’t just convenience; it’s a scaffold for consistency.

The counterintuitive truth? Resisting distraction during meditation sharpens your ability to dive deep when you choose to. Similarly, building friction into personal systems—like requiring a right swipe to publish a social post—forces intentionality. These constraints aren’t obstacles; they’re the architecture of mastery.

This principle is most visible when building tools for oneself. In developing my "slow clock" app, I've realized that the interface is more than just a UI—it is a reflection of how I process thought. When a system allows for seamless journaling that can later be distilled into a blog post, a video clip, or a "core memory" point, it ceases to be a temporary fix and becomes a digital persona. The tension lies in the transition from capture to output: how do we move from the raw stream of consciousness in a journal to a structured, public-facing output without losing the essence of the original spark?

New considerations emerge from my work on a cross-platform app (Mac, iOS, Windows, Android) built with Rust/Tori for simplicity and minimal effort across platforms. The design prioritizes processing digital inputs (text, audio, video) into curated outputs—like social media posts or video edits—while using vectorization to align content with personal relevance. This approach mirrors company-scale infrastructure: systems must adapt to diverse contexts (platforms, user needs) while maintaining a core purpose.

Another tension arises in curation. By vectorizing my digital inputs, I aim to surface content that matches my interests or contrasts meaningfully—a foundation for a community-driven feed algorithm. This requires balancing individual control (personal "vector list") with scalable, shared tools. How do personal systems evolve when they invite collective input, much like open-source company systems?

Building Starts By Designing For One Real User

Hermes Agent’s skills, OpenClock’s integration, and the potential skill hub remain central to the design conversation. Tensions persist in balancing automation with user control. The chat tab’s ability to auto‑generate and schedule reminders via AI agents showcases automation’s power, yet the fragility of systems that depend on ambiguous instructions—such as an agent stuck trying to send a script instead of executing it—highlights the need for precise intent interpretation. This mirrors the broader challenge of designing systems that infer user intent without overstepping.

Examples include the slow claw app’s chat section, where AI crafts stories for scheduled reminders, bridging creative and functional tasks. However, unclear prompts like “create a script” expose a tension between flexibility and precision; when the agent cannot distinguish generation from execution, users must debug a process that should feel seamless. Scalability questions arise: can such systems adapt to diverse workflows without becoming brittle, and what ethical concerns emerge when AI autonomously executes tasks from partial input? The frustration of a chatbot that “almost” works underscores the need for clearer boundaries between conversational interfaces and action‑oriented tools.

New insights from recent discussions emphasize that we are at the end of AI’s exponential growth, a tectonic shift that makes it an unprecedented time to be a builder. A cousin’s project around taste‑based community recommendations illustrates how user‑centric design can surface relevant content, while the observation that humans can hold an entire problem in mind and break it into incremental steps—something current AI cannot fully replicate—suggests a design imperative to preserve holistic cognition. The notion of a single person managing three monitors, with agents orchestrating tasks across them, points to a future where personal AI agents coordinate multiple streams of work, yet the context space remains a critical bottleneck.

In my own workflow, daily audio recordings and the desire for an AI to process them, test outcomes, and provide iterative feedback align with a vision of work where every individual has a GPU‑powered AI companion on their phone or laptop. This companion would handle routine tasks, freeing the user to meditate, reflect, and iteratively improve their output. However, the challenge lies in defining clear boundaries: the AI must act on behalf of the user without usurping agency, and it must respect the user’s timeline and contextual nuances. Designing for one real user therefore demands systems that blend precise execution with flexible, context‑aware orchestration, ensuring that automation enhances rather than obscures the user’s intentional control.
