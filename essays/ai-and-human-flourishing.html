AI and Human Flourishing

The most compelling question about artificial intelligence today isn't whether the models are impressive—it's whether they actually expand our capacity to think, act, and cooperate. In my notes from recent months, I keep returning to a single fork in the road: AI can become a prosthetic for human agency, or it can settle in as another layer of dependence that quietly narrows the space of our lives.

Intelligence Should Lower Friction for Thought

AI can become the “mental grease” that lets ideas slide from spark to insight without the usual detours. A concrete illustration comes from a recent conference‑tech prototype: an on‑device journal that records audio, chunks the transcript, and creates local embeddings. The system then surfaces similarity scores and auto‑generated questions that push participants toward deeper connections with the material and each other—without ever sending raw notes to the cloud. By handling privacy locally, the tool sidesteps the friction many feel about exposing personal reflections online, while still delivering the cognitive lift that a human‑only note‑taking process lacks.

The tension lies in balancing accessibility (any smartphone can run the model) with mastery (users must trust and understand the AI’s suggestions). If the interface is too opaque, participants may ignore the generated prompts, reverting to the slower, manual synthesis they already know. Conversely, if the AI is overly prescriptive, it risks narrowing thought rather than expanding it, turning a potential catalyst for curiosity into a subtle form of intellectual friction.

Another layer of friction appears in the social fabric of conferences themselves. Traditional formats rely on chance encounters in hallways or scheduled coffee breaks, which often leave many participants disconnected. Embedding AI‑driven recommendation engines that match attendees based on the semantic content of their journal entries could deliberately engineer serendipity, lowering the social friction that currently limits fruitful dialogue.

The open question, then, is not whether AI can reduce the mechanical obstacles to thinking, but how we design those reductions so they amplify human agency rather than replace it. Should the AI surface only the most relevant prompts, or also expose the less obvious, “root‑nose” problems that participants might never have considered? The answer will shape whether intelligence truly becomes a low‑friction conduit for flourishing thought.

The Economic Question Cannot Be Dodged

Technical power alone does not answer social questions. If intelligence becomes cheap while ownership stays concentrated, we risk a new kind of inequality: abundance on one layer (computational capacity) but scarcity on another (economic leverage). This realization has kept pulling my attention from model performance to institutional design—zero-profit food systems, humane coordination structures, and other alternatives that aim to prevent AI from merely extracting more value from existing power structures.

These ideas are not side quests; they are attempts to ask what we should actually build if AI reorganizes who gets leverage, who gets displaced, and who sets the terms of collective life. The economic architecture surrounding AI must evolve in tandem with the technology, or we will simply replace one set of monopolies with another.

Agency Is the Real Metric

The strongest standard that emerges from my reflections is simple: tools should increase human agency. An AI system ought to help a person manage information, recover context, follow through on intentions, and coordinate with others more intelligently. When an AI merely produces outputs while leaving the user more passive, fragmented, or dependent on opaque processes, it has failed the ethical test.

Designing for agency shifts the focus from automation for its own sake to intelligence that leaves people freer, steadier, and more able to act with clarity. It also forces us to ask concrete design questions: what would a product look like if human agency were the primary metric? How can AI improve coordination without concentrating power in the same old institutions?

Open Questions

  • What would an AI product look like if human agency were the primary design metric?
  • Which economic arrangements become necessary if intelligence grows more abundant than meaningful work?
  • How can AI improve coordination without concentrating power in the same old institutions?
  • What would an AI system look like if its main goal were to increase human agency?
  • Which economic structures become necessary if intelligence becomes abundant but unevenly distributed?
  • How do better communication tools change how people coordinate, learn, and govern themselves?
