Awareness As A Daily Practice

The core of this practice is lovingly welcoming everything—family, body, each moment—as teacher. My grandfather’s Raja‑Yoga room hints at an ancestral lineage of mindfulness, yet the ego often pulls me toward stimulation and superiority; I notice a habit of provoking reactions for a perverse thrill, revealing a tension between witness and actor. Desiring likes or validation—even after coming out—exposes the ego’s fragility, inviting me to see the universal witness in others and question the parts I mistake for identity.

Recent reflections add a practical tension: balancing family life with work on slowclaw.social sparked a clash when Rika highlighted priorities, echoing Vivekananda’s advice to “attach and detach at will.” He illustrated this with the metaphor of lappam—a material that adheres strongly yet releases easily when broken—emphasizing that true freedom lies in the ability to choose attachment or detachment. This insight aligns with Advaita Vedanta’s warning about wrong identification; we constantly mislabel the ever‑changing self, as illustrated by the balcony scene of a father chasing his daughter, his anger masking a deeper need to protect while also learning to let go. The suffering of the universe, I realize, stems from an inability to detach at will, a lesson that surfaces in daily irritations and in the yearning for social approval.

True awareness means recognizing that superiority—whether coaching parents or judging others—is an illusion. Every interaction, even boring or irritating ones, becomes meditation, a mirror that reflects the ego’s projections. The self is fluid; who I was years ago differs from who I am now, and each shift invites questioning what I cling to for pride. Even mundane moments—running by the river, observing triggers as a panorama—offer chances to practice detached presence, whether in meditation, in building a slow‑moving social tool, or in surrendering the deepest craving for breath. In this way, the practice remains a gymnasium for the soul, where each breath, each attachment, and each release refines ojas and brings us closer to the truth that we are already enough—Sat Chidānanda.

Acceptance Without Passivity

In Vipassana the instruction is not to resist or despise uncomfortable sensations, but to welcome them as gifts that arise for us in the present moment. When a tightness in the chest or a wave of anxiety appears, the practice invites us to notice it, name it, and let it be, rather than demanding that it never occur because we “should” be a seasoned meditator. This shift turns acceptance from a passive resignation into an active, compassionate inquiry.

The experience described in the tram—watching a meeting agenda unfold, seeing how a small group of seven people could launch a thriving half‑day course—illustrates another facet of this active acceptance. The center’s structure is deliberately independent and self‑organizing, with transparent principles available online. Knowing that such a community can arise from a simple, shared intention reminds us that we do not need external validation to be whole; the capacity to create is already present within us.

At the same time, the notes reveal a subtle tension: the inner voice that says “I should not feel this” is often the egoic self, not the true experiencer. Recognizing that the “I” in self‑talk is a mental construct allows us to separate judgment from observation. We can then engage the sensation fully—using it as material for insight—without being driven by the ego’s demand for a particular outcome.

Thus, acceptance without passivity becomes a dynamic balance:

  • Seeing sensations as transient teachers, not as commands to act or as failures of practice.
  • Acknowledging our inherent completeness, which frees us from needing external approval while still participating fully in communal efforts like the Vipassana center.
  • Identifying the egoic narrative, letting it fall away so that clear, loving awareness can arise.

In this way, we meet each moment with “loving welcome, loving letting go,” remaining anchored in our unchanging wholeness while actively shaping our lives and communities.
