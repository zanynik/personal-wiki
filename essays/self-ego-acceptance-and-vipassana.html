Awareness As A Daily Practice

The core of this practice is the act of lovingly welcoming everything: the family I was born into, the body I have been given, and the immediate experience of every moment, treating each as a teacher. This lineage of mindfulness may be ancestral; my grandfather maintained a meditation room and followed Raja Yoga and Mount Abu-based practices, suggesting a deep-seated familial predisposition toward this path.

However, this practice often clashes with the ego's desire for stimulation and superiority. I have noticed a habit of initiating conversations specifically to trigger reactions in others, finding a certain perverse enjoyment in the resulting friction. This reveals a tension between the role of the "witness" and the role of the "actor."

True awareness requires recognizing that when we feel superior—whether we are coaching our parents or judging those around us—we are merely attaching ourselves to an illusion. If I believe I am "better" or "different," I am failing to see the universal witness in others. Every interaction, every boring situation, and every irritating conversation is actually a form of meditation. The illusions others project through their actions and presence are mirrors, designed to help me question the parts of myself that I mistake for my identity.

Ultimately, the ego is a collection of memories and mental conditioning, but the "I" is an ever-changing phenomenon. Who I was years ago is not who I am now. By recognizing that the self is not a fixed entity but a shifting experience, I can begin to question what exactly I am trying to be proud of. Boredom or superiority are simply signals that I am not fully present; they are reminders that I have forgotten my position as the witness and have instead become entangled in the illusion of the ego.

Acceptance Without Passivity

True acceptance is not passive surrender but an active engagement with life as it unfolds. The wisdom traditions teach us that we are inherently complete, whole in ourselves, needing nothing external to validate our existence. This fundamental understanding liberates us from being slaves to circumstance or material possessions.

When sensations arise in the body and feelings of incompleteness emerge, we can recognize them as transient phenomena rather than commands to act. The Vedantic insight that we are complete by ourselves allows us to observe these sensations without being controlled by them. Nothing that comes has the power to diminish our inherent happiness or completeness.

This active acceptance means welcoming all situations with loving wisdom—understanding that all things come and go. It's not about passively allowing life to happen to us, but about meeting each moment with clarity and presence. The practice is one of "loving welcome, loving letting go," maintaining awareness of our true nature while fully participating in the dance of life. Yoga, at its essence, is this union of our temporal self with the eternal, allowing us to engage with the world while remaining anchored in our unchanging completeness.
