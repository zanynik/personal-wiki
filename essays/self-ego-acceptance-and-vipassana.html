Awareness As A Daily Practice

The core of this practice is the act of lovingly welcoming everything: the family I was born into, the body I have been given, and the immediate experience of every moment, treating each as a teacher. This lineage of mindfulness may be ancestral; my grandfather maintained a meditation room and followed Raja Yoga and Mount Abu-based practices, suggesting a deep-seated familial predisposition toward this path.

However, this practice often clashes with the ego's desire for stimulation and superiority. I have noticed a habit of initiating conversations specifically to trigger reactions in others, finding a certain perverse enjoyment in the resulting friction. This reveals a tension between the role of the "witness" and the role of the "actor."

True awareness requires recognizing that when we feel superior—whether we are coaching our parents or judging those around us—we are merely attaching ourselves to an illusion. If I believe I am "better" or "different," I am failing to see the universal witness in others. Every interaction, every boring situation, and every irritating conversation is actually a form of meditation. The illusions others project through their actions and presence are mirrors, designed to help me question the parts of myself that I mistake for my identity.

One recurring insight from my morning practice involves the transformation of deep-seated energies. After reading Vivekananda’s writings on Sri Ramakrishna—just before settling into meditation—I’ve been reflecting on how lustful dreams and bodily compulsions can be transmuted into creative force. Sex, as a non-survival compulsion rooted in evolution’s depths, represents a primal enslavement. Yet its conquest is possible: mastering this urge unlocks broader control over the nervous system, revealing it as the first hurdle in transcending causal entanglement. The energy once channeled into desire becomes ojas—a refined, productive vitality.

This aligns with the broader understanding that the ego clings to fixed identities, while awareness remains fluid. Who I was years ago is not who I am now. By recognizing that the self is not a fixed entity but a shifting experience, I can begin to question what exactly I am trying to be proud of. Boredom or superiority are simply signals that I am not fully present; they are reminders that I have forgotten my position as the witness and have instead become entangled in the illusion of the ego.

Even mundane moments—like running along the river at dawn, observing triggers as a panorama—become opportunities to practice this panorama of awareness. The slow cloud of social apps I envision building also demands this same presence: each interaction a chance to witness without attachment, to see beyond the ego’s projections. Yet I grapple with the ethics of such tools, knowing that privacy and consent must remain sacred, even as technology tempts us to capture and share without reflection.

Acceptance Without Passivity

In Vipassana the instruction is not to resist or despise uncomfortable sensations, but to welcome them as gifts that arise for us in the present moment. When a tightness in the chest or a wave of anxiety appears, the practice invites us to notice it, name it, and let it be, rather than demanding that it never occur because we “should” be a seasoned meditator. This shift turns acceptance from a passive resignation into an active, compassionate inquiry.

The experience described in the tram—watching a meeting agenda unfold, seeing how a small group of seven people could launch a thriving half‑day course—illustrates another facet of this active acceptance. The center’s structure is deliberately independent and self‑organizing, with transparent principles available online. Knowing that such a community can arise from a simple, shared intention reminds us that we do not need external validation to be whole; the capacity to create is already present within us.

At the same time, the notes reveal a subtle tension: the inner voice that says “I should not feel this” is often the egoic self, not the true experiencer. Recognizing that the “I” in self‑talk is a mental construct allows us to separate judgment from observation. We can then engage the sensation fully—using it as material for insight—without being driven by the ego’s demand for a particular outcome.

Thus, acceptance without passivity becomes a dynamic balance:

  • Seeing sensations as transient teachers, not as commands to act or as failures of practice.
  • Acknowledging our inherent completeness, which frees us from needing external approval while still participating fully in communal efforts like the Vipassana center.
  • Identifying the egoic narrative, letting it fall away so that clear, loving awareness can arise.

In this way, we meet each moment with “loving welcome, loving letting go,” remaining anchored in our unchanging wholeness while actively shaping our lives and communities.
